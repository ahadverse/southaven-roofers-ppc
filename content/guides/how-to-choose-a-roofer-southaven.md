## Start with the Mississippi contractor license

### When a state license is required

Residential roofers in Mississippi are licensed by the **Mississippi State Board of Contractors (MSBOC)**. Under the state's residential builders and remodelers law (Miss. Code § 73-59-1), a "remodeler" is anyone paid to improve an existing home when the total cost is **more than $10,000**. The board's rules create a **residential roofer** subclassification of the remodeler license, with its own roofer's exam. The board's FAQ says it directly: residential roofing over $10,000 requires a license.

- **Many replacements cross the line; many repairs don't.** This Old House puts the average Mississippi roof replacement at about $7,763, so smaller jobs can legally be done without a state residential license. Ask for one anyway. A licensed contractor has passed the board's exam and filed proof of general liability insurance and, where applicable, workers' compensation.
- **Working without a required license is a misdemeanor**, with a fine of $100 to $5,000, 30 to 60 days in county jail, or both. An unlicensed remodeler also can't go to court to enforce the contract.

### How to look up a roofer on the MSBOC website

1. Go to **msboc.us** and choose **Search Contractors** (some menus call it **Check A License**).
2. Search by **name or license number**. For license numbers, enter the digits only, without the letter prefix or suffix. An Advanced Search option is also available.
3. Confirm that the **name on the license matches the name on your estimate and contract**, that it's current, and that it covers residential remodeling or roofing.
4. If anything looks off, **call the board at 800-880-6161**. The search page notes that its data may not be current and suggests contacting the office before you decide.

The board also warns that scam texts and emails sometimes impersonate MSBOC, so type the address in yourself.

## Get proof of insurance straight from the insurer

Ask about two kinds of coverage:

- **General liability** can respond if the contractor's work damages your home or a neighbor's property.
- **Workers' compensation** covers injuries to the contractor's workers. The licensing law requires proof of workers' comp only "if applicable." If a company says it isn't required, ask how its workers and any subcontracted crews are covered, and run it by your own insurance agent.

The Mississippi Attorney General's storm guide recommends requesting a **certificate of insurance** and verifying it with whoever issued it. The cleanest way: have the contractor's insurance agent **send the certificate directly to you**, then call that agency to confirm the policy is active. A PDF forwarded by a salesperson can be expired or altered.

There's a built-in check, too. State rules require a licensed residential builder, remodeler or roofer to print a liability insurance disclosure **immediately above your signature line** on the contract, in bold type larger than the rest, naming the insurer and policy number. If it's missing, ask why.

## Look for local presence, reviews and references

Southaven sits on the Tennessee line, so **a Tennessee plate means little by itself.** Plenty of established Memphis-area companies work both sides of Stateline Road. What matters is a Mississippi license for jobs over $10,000, a business address you can verify, and a track record. Out-of-state contractors can get licensed here, but they still have to apply and meet MSBOC's requirements, and an out-of-state corporation must qualify with the Mississippi Secretary of State.

When you check a roofer's reputation:

- **Read reviews on more than one platform** and look at the dates. A burst of reviews right after a storm, with nothing before, is worth noticing.
- **Ask for references.** MSBOC suggests at least three written references; ask whether the contractor kept to the schedule and followed the contract. Recent jobs in neighborhoods like Greenbrook, Deerchase or Central Gardens are easy to drive by.
- **Find out who will be on your roof.** Subcontracted crews are common, but you should know who supervises and who to call if something goes wrong.

## What a solid roofing contract should include

The Attorney General's storm guide says to require a written contract covering the work, materials, a payment schedule based on completed work, and a timeline (it even includes a model contract). Changes should be in writing and initialed by both of you. To compare prices and line items across bids, use our [roof replacement cost guide](/roof-replacement-cost-southaven).

| Contract item | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Scope of work | Tear-off or a defined repair area, layers removed, underlayment, flashing, vents, drip edge |
| Materials | Brand, product line, color and quantity; if you don't specify quality, the contractor decides |
| Decking | Price per 4x8 sheet, sheets included, and photos of any decking replaced |
| Permits | Who pulls the permit and whether the fee is included |
| Cleanup | Dumpster placement, landscaping protection, haul-away and a magnet sweep for nails |
| Payment schedule | Amounts tied to completed stages, with a small (or no) deposit |
| Start and finish dates | Projected start, expected duration and how weather delays are handled |
| Warranties | Manufacturer and workmanship coverage in writing, with length and who backs each |
| Cancellation rights | How and when you can cancel (see below) |
| Lien waivers | A commitment to provide waivers or proof that suppliers and subcontractors were paid |
| Insurance-funded extras | On insurance jobs, a written list of any work beyond the covered damage that you'll pay for |

### Lien waivers, explained

Generally, contractors, subcontractors and suppliers who aren't paid for work on a home can file a **lien**, a legal claim against the property. That can happen even after you've paid your roofer in full, if the roofer never paid the supply house. A **lien waiver** (or release) is a signed document in which one of those parties gives up its lien rights for the amount it has been paid. Some are conditional, taking effect only once your payment clears.

The Attorney General recommends having the contractor confirm that all subcontractors and suppliers were paid before your final payment, and MSBOC advises paying the final amount only after verifying that liens have been released. If a lien is filed, talk to an attorney.

### Cancellation rights

- **Sales made at your home.** The FTC's Cooling-Off Rule generally lets you cancel certain sales of $25 or more made at your home until midnight of the third business day. Exceptions include sales needed to meet an emergency and repairs you invited the seller over to do, so it may not cover a roofer you called yourself.
- **Insurance-funded roof contracts.** Under Mississippi law, the contract must come with a bold-type statement and a detachable Notice of Cancellation form saying you may cancel within **three business days after you receive written notice from your insurer** that all or part of your claim isn't covered. If you cancel, payments must be returned within 10 business days, except for emergency services and completed repairs your insurer later approved.

## Manufacturer certifications vs. workmanship warranties

Sales pitches blur these together, but they're different:

- **A manufacturer's warranty** covers shingles and other products against manufacturing defects. Standard product warranties generally don't pay for installation mistakes.
- **A workmanship warranty** is the contractor's promise about the installation: flashing, nailing, vents and the leaks that come from getting them wrong. It's only as good as the company behind it. A 10-year workmanship warranty from a crew that leaves after storm season won't help much in year three.
- **A manufacturer certification** means the contractor met a shingle maker's program requirements. Certified contractors can often offer enhanced "system" warranties with some manufacturer-backed workmanship coverage, typically only when the roof uses that manufacturer's components and the warranty is registered. Terms vary widely.

Confirm any certification on the manufacturer's website, ask for the actual warranty document, and ask who registers it. A certification comes from a company, not the state; it doesn't replace a Mississippi license.

## Permits, deposits and payment schedules

### Permits in Southaven

The **City of Southaven Building Department** handles permits, plan review and inspections, uses the 2018 International Code Council family of codes, and offers an online self-service permit portal. Whether your job needs a permit depends on the scope, so ask your contractor and confirm with the department.

The Attorney General's guide says the contractor should pull any required permits, and that you should confirm with the permit office before work begins. Be wary of a contractor who asks you to pull it yourself; MSBOC warns that unlicensed contractors pitch that as a money saver. Once a permit is issued, state rules require the licensed contractor to post a job-site sign showing its license number.

### Deposits and payment schedules

- **Don't pay in full up front.** The Attorney General notes that reputable contractors can usually buy materials on account, and that a required deposit shouldn't exceed 10% or 25% of the total cost.
- **Pay as work is completed**, tying each payment to a finished stage you've seen.
- **Pay by check or money order made out to the company**, not an individual worker, and keep receipts. Avoid cash.
- **On insurance jobs**, Mississippi law says the contractor can't require any payment from you until the three-day cancellation period has expired. Match the rest of the schedule to when insurance checks arrive; our [roof insurance claim help](/roof-insurance-claims) page explains how those payments usually come in stages.
- **Hold the final payment** until the walkthrough is done, any required inspection has passed and you have lien waivers.

## Storm chaser red flags

After a big hail or wind event, out-of-town crews often canvass Southaven neighborhoods. Some do fine work, but the more of these signs you see together, the more careful you should be:

- **Door-to-door solicitation.** The Attorney General specifically warns about door-to-door repair offers. Anyone who calls your roof "definitely damaged" before inspecting it is selling.
- **Pressure to sign today**, or "sign this so we can talk to your insurance company." That paper may be a contract or an assignment of benefits.
- **No local address**, just a cell number and a vague answer about where the company is based.
- **Cash only, or a large deposit.** The Attorney General warns about people who demand deposits or payments in cash.
- **An offer to pay, waive or "absorb" your deductible.** Under HB 1408 (2024), a residential roofing contractor on an insurance-funded job can't advertise, promise, offer to pay, pay or rebate any part of your deductible as an inducement to sign.
- **An "assignment of benefits" on the spot.** State law limits a post-loss assignment to naming the contractor as a **co-payee** on insurance payments, and requires itemized work, materials, labor and fees, a total, and a capitalized notice that you're giving up certain rights under your policy. If that's missing, don't sign.
- **An offer to negotiate your claim before you've filed it.** Until you file, a roofing contractor can't represent or negotiate your claim, or offer to. (Licensed public adjusters are exempt; check a license on the Mississippi Insurance Department's licensing search.) A contractor can still write an estimate and talk with your adjuster.
- **Pushing a particular lawyer.** The same law bars roofing contractors from taking anything of value from an attorney for claim referrals or obligating you to hire a specific attorney.

Violations count as an unfair and deceptive practice under the Mississippi Consumer Protection Law, with civil and criminal penalties. ## What to do if something goes wrong

1. **Put the problem in writing to the contractor.** Reference the contract, attach photos and keep copies.
2. **Stop any payment that isn't due**, and hold the final payment until it's resolved.
3. **File a complaint with MSBOC**, online with file uploads or by mail on its fillable form (send copies, not originals). The board covers licensed and unlicensed contractors and issues like poor workmanship, abandonment and unpaid subcontractors or suppliers. It can fine a contractor or suspend or revoke a license, but an investigation doesn't mean you'll get your money back; for restitution, the board points you to the courts or an attorney.
4. **Contact the Attorney General's Consumer Protection Division** through its online complaint form, by email at consumer@ago.ms.gov or at 1-800-281-4418.
5. **For claim problems, call your insurer and agent**, and log every conversation.
6. **Talk to an attorney** about contract disputes, liens or significant sums.
