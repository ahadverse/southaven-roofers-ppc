Hail is part of life in DeSoto County. One minute it's a warm spring afternoon, the next the sky goes green and ice is bouncing off the driveway. When it stops, most homeowners have the same question: **what now?**

What you do in the first hour, the first day and the first week after a hail storm affects how safely you get through it, how much extra damage you prevent, and how well any insurance claim holds up. This checklist walks through each step for Southaven homeowners, with a printable table at the end.

## How often hail hits Southaven

Hail reports near Southaven show a clear pattern. According to StormerSite, which compiles NOAA Storm Prediction Center reports, **April has historically been the most active month for hail** around the city, and most reports are **quarter-sized (about 1 inch) or smaller**. Larger stones do happen:

- **January 25, 2021:** NOAA storm reports include golf-ball-size hail (1.75 inches) near Stateline Road in Southaven, a reminder that hail isn't only a spring event.
- **April 29, 2025:** quarter-size (1-inch) hail was reported near College Road, south-southeast of the city center.

For context, the National Weather Service considers a thunderstorm severe when it can produce hail **an inch or larger** or wind gusts over 58 mph. In other words, hail doesn't have to be golf-ball size to matter: quarter-size hail already meets the severe threshold, and it can damage older or worn shingles.

## The first hour: stay safe and capture the evidence

The storm may have moved on, but this is when people get hurt. Work through these in order:

1. **Wait until the storm has fully passed.** Lightning and a second round of hail can trail behind the first.
2. **Check for urgent hazards.** Downed power lines, broken windows, a tree on the house or water pouring through a ceiling come before anything else. Keep everyone away from damaged areas and call 911 or your utility if lines are down.
3. **Do not climb onto the roof.** Wet shingles, loose granules and hidden soft spots make a storm-damaged roof dangerous, even for people who are comfortable on ladders.
4. **Photograph the hail before it melts.** Set a few stones next to a **coin or ruler** and take close-up photos. A quarter is about 1 inch across, which gives an adjuster an instant sense of scale. If hail is piled up, photograph that too.
5. **Note the date and time.** Write down when the hail started and stopped, and screenshot any weather alerts on your phone. You may need that date months from now.

## The first day: check everything you can from the ground

Once it's safe, walk around the outside of your home with your phone. You're not diagnosing the roof (that comes later). You're gathering clues and **photographing everything with something for scale** in the frame.

### Outside the house

- **Gutters and downspouts:** look for fresh dents, and check the splash area under each downspout for piles of dark, sand-like granules.
- **AC condenser:** run your fingers lightly along the aluminum fins. Rows of small dents often show hail size and direction.
- **Siding and trim:** cracks or holes in vinyl, chips in painted wood, and dents in metal trim or window wraps.
- **Window screens:** small tears or stretched spots are easy to miss and easy to photograph.
- **Fences, decks and patio furniture:** fresh "spatter" marks where hail knocked off grime or paint.
- **Vehicles:** dents on the hood and roof are strong evidence of hail size. If a car was damaged, call your auto insurer separately.
- **The yard:** pieces of shingle, granules on walkways or bits of vent cap mean the roof took hits.

### Inside the house and attic

- Look at ceilings in every room, especially top-floor rooms and closets, for **new stains or drips**.
- Check around windows and skylights, since wind-driven rain often sneaks in there first.
- If you can safely reach the attic, use a flashlight to look for **wet insulation, drips along nails or daylight** through the deck. Step only on framing, never on the drywall between joists.

### If water is coming in

Put buckets and towels under drips, move furniture and electronics out of the way, and keep people out of any room where the ceiling is sagging. Then call for an [emergency roof repair](/emergency-roof-repair) such as professional tarping. Most policies expect you to take reasonable steps to prevent further damage, so **save every receipt** for tarps, buckets or emergency service.

## The first week: inspection, insurance and protection

The days after a storm are about getting facts and making decisions in the right order.

1. **Schedule a free roof inspection.** A roofer can check every slope up close, mark hail hits, and photograph damage you'll never see from the yard. Our [roof inspection](/roof-inspection) page explains what a thorough inspection covers, and our [hail damage roof repair](/hail-damage-roof-repair) page shows what bruised shingles and dented vents look like up close.
2. **Decide whether to file a claim.** Compare what the inspection found with your deductible. Many policies have a separate wind/hail deductible, sometimes a percentage of your dwelling coverage. If the damage is minor and well under that amount, a claim may not make sense.
3. **Contact your insurer if the damage warrants it.** Report the storm date, get a claim number, and ask when the adjuster will visit. Keep a simple log of every call: the date, who you spoke with and what they said.
4. **Keep protecting the house.** Make sure any tarp stays secure through the next rain. Hold off on permanent repairs until the adjuster has seen the damage, unless your insurer tells you otherwise.
5. **Be there for the adjuster's visit,** ideally with your roofer, so vents, flashing, gutters and other damage aren't missed.

## Dealing with door-to-door contractors after a storm

Within days of a big hail storm, Southaven streets can fill with yard signs and contractors knocking on doors. Some are legitimate. Others aren't licensed, aren't local, and won't be around when a warranty problem shows up. The Federal Trade Commission warns that unlicensed contractors and scammers often appear after weather emergencies, and its advice applies here:

- **If they want cash up front, walk away.**
- **Get a written contract** and read it carefully before you sign.
- **Confirm the contractor's license and insurance.** You can check a Mississippi license on the **Mississippi State Board of Contractors** website.
- **Pay by credit card or check,** never by wire transfer, gift card, payment app, cryptocurrency or cash.
- **Don't make the final payment** until the work is done and you're satisfied.

Mississippi law adds protections for roofing work paid for by insurance. Under the state's Insurance Benefits Roofing Repair Consumer Protection Act, as amended in 2024:

- A residential roofing contractor **may not offer to pay or rebate any part of your deductible** to win the job.
- A roofer **can't negotiate your claim on your behalf** before you've filed it (licensed public adjusters are a separate category).
- Insurance-funded roofing contracts must tell you that you can **cancel within three business days** after your insurer notifies you in writing that all or part of the claim isn't covered, and the roofer **can't require payment** until that three-day period has passed.

This is general information, not legal advice. Read your contract and check the law or ask an attorney if you have questions. Before you sign anything, read [how to choose a roofer in Southaven](/how-to-choose-a-roofer-southaven).
