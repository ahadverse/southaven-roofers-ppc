Every spring, the Mid-South gets its share of severe weather: lines of thunderstorms with straight-line winds, hail and tornado watches. Southaven roofs take the brunt of it, and so do the big trees that shade so many of the city's established neighborhoods.

Storms don't keep to a calendar, either. On **July 13, 2025**, a pop-up afternoon storm brought down multiple trees across Southaven, according to NOAA Storm Prediction Center reports. Local news coverage said the National Weather Service reported wind damage, with gusts possibly reaching 50 to 60 mph, plus downed trees and a power pole near Hamilton Road and Stateline Road. No hail was reported, just wind, and it was enough.

This checklist covers what to do before storm season, during a storm and after one passes, plus a printable maintenance table and clear signs it's time to call a professional.

## When storm season hits the Mid-South

The National Weather Service office in Memphis, which covers DeSoto County, has studied severe weather across its forecast area. Its climatology finds that:

- **Spring into early summer is the main season.** The traditional season is thought of as March through May, but the most active stretch actually runs from **April through June**.
- **Hail** is most frequent from March through June.
- **Damaging wind** peaks in June, which lines up with the hot-weather pop-up storms many Southaven residents know well.
- **There's a smaller secondary peak in November.** The office notes a secondary maximum for wind and hail reports in November, and a secondary "tornado season" in November and December.

The practical takeaway: do your main roof prep in **late winter**, before March, and do a shorter second round in **October**, before the late-fall storms.

## Before the season: your roof prep checklist

### Clean gutters and downspouts

Clogged gutters overflow during heavy downpours, sending water behind fascia boards and down foundation walls. Clear out leaves, pine needles and shingle granules, and flush each downspout with a hose to make sure water flows freely. Make sure splash blocks or extensions carry water away from the house.

### Trim limbs that hang over the roof

Southaven has a lot of mature tree cover, and overhanging branches are one of the most common causes of storm damage. In high wind, limbs scrape shingles, break off and puncture the roof deck. As a rule of thumb, keep branches well clear of the roof surface. Hire a tree service for large limbs, and call your utility about anything near power lines.

### Check flashing, pipe boots and vents

You don't need to climb a ladder for this. From the ground, a pair of binoculars works well. Look for:

- **Cracked or split rubber pipe boots** around plumbing vents
- **Loose or rusted flashing** at chimneys, walls and skylights
- **Lifted, curled or missing shingles,** especially at ridges and roof edges
- **Dented or loose vent caps**

If something looks off, have a roofer check it up close before the first big storm.

### Secure anything that can fly

Patio furniture, grills, trampolines, kids' toys and loose fence boards can all turn into projectiles in straight-line winds. Store what you can, and anchor what you can't.

### Photograph your roof's current condition

Walk around the house and take clear photos of every roof slope you can see, plus the gutters, siding and attic. Save them somewhere safe, like a cloud folder, with the date. If a storm damages your roof later, these "before" photos help show what's new.

### Take a quick look in the attic

On a dry day, grab a flashlight and check the underside of the roof deck. Old water stains, damp or matted insulation, rusty nail tips and pinpoints of daylight all point to problems that a storm will make worse. Note what you find and photograph it, so you'll know later whether a stain is new. If the attic feels like an oven on a summer afternoon, mention it to a roofer too: poor ventilation ages shingles faster and leaves them more brittle when the next storm hits.

### Review your insurance policy

Pull out your homeowners policy, or call your agent, and check:

- Your **wind/hail deductible**, which may be a percentage of your dwelling coverage rather than a flat amount
- Whether your roof is covered at **replacement cost or actual cash value**
- The **deadline for reporting** storm damage
- Whether **emergency repairs** like tarping are reimbursed

## During a storm: safety comes first

No roof is worth getting hurt over. When severe weather is in the forecast:

- **Know the difference between a watch and a warning.** The National Weather Service says a severe thunderstorm **watch** means severe storms are possible in and near the watch area. A **warning** means imminent danger to life and property.
- **Have more than one way to get warnings,** such as phone alerts, a weather radio and local TV or radio, especially overnight.
- **During a severe thunderstorm warning,** go to your secure location and stay away from windows.
- **During a tornado warning,** the NWS advises going to a basement, safe room or an interior room away from windows.
- **When thunder roars, go indoors.** That's the NWS rule of thumb for lightning, and it applies to cleanup too.
- **Never go on the roof during a storm** to fix a leak. Put a bucket under the drip and wait for the weather to clear.

## After the storm: inspect and document

Once the storm has passed and it's safe to go outside, work through these steps:

1. **Check for hazards.** Stay away from downed power lines, and keep out of rooms with sagging ceilings.
2. **Look at the roof from the ground.** Missing shingles, exposed underlayment, creased tabs, debris on the roof and bent gutters are all signs of damage.
3. **Check inside.** Look at ceilings, around windows and, if it's safe, in the attic for drips or wet insulation.
4. **Document everything.** Take date-stamped photos and video, and compare them with your pre-season photos. Write down the storm's date and time.
5. **Prevent further damage.** If water is getting in, call for [emergency roof repair](/emergency-roof-repair) or tarping, and keep every receipt.
6. **Get a professional inspection.** Wind damage like broken shingle seals is often invisible from the yard. ## Printable seasonal roof maintenance table

| Season | Roof and yard tasks |
|---|---|
| Late winter (Feb to early Mar) | Clean gutters; trim overhanging limbs; check pipe boots, flashing and vents with binoculars; photograph the roof; review your policy and deductible |
| Spring (Mar to Jun) | Secure outdoor items when storms are forecast; check the roof and gutters from the ground after every severe storm; keep a roofer's number handy |
| Summer (Jul to Aug) | Watch for pop-up storm damage; check attic ventilation and look for signs of heat stress such as curling shingles |
| Fall (Sep to Nov) | Clean gutters after leaves drop; do a second limb check; re-photograph the roof before the November storm bump |
| Winter (Dec to Jan) | Check ceilings and the attic after heavy rain; schedule an inspection if your roof is older or took storm hits |
| After any severe storm | Stay safe, check from the ground, document damage, tarp if needed, and get an inspection |

## When to call a pro

Some roof care is a homeowner job. Anything that requires climbing on the roof is not. Call a roofer when you notice:

- **Missing, lifted or creased shingles,** even just a few
- **A new leak** or water stain, no matter how small
- **Granules piling up** in gutters or at downspouts
- **Damaged flashing, pipe boots or vents**
- **A limb or tree** that hit the roof, even if it rolled off
- **A roof older than about 15 years** heading into storm season

A [professional roof inspection](/roof-inspection) gives you a clear picture of where your roof stands and what, if anything, needs attention.
