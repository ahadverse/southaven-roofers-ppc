When a roof starts giving you trouble, the big question is whether to fix the problem or start over. Get it wrong one way and you spend thousands of dollars before you need to. Get it wrong the other way and you keep paying for patches on a roof that's worn out.

There's no single rule that fits every Southaven home, but there is a sensible way to think it through. This guide walks you through the decision the way an honest roofer would, so you can ask better questions and recognize a fair recommendation when you hear one.

## The decision framework: seven questions

### 1. How old is the roof?

Age is the starting point. Architectural shingles typically last roughly 20 to 30 years, and three-tab shingles often less. A roof in its first 10 to 15 years usually deserves a repair. A roof past 20 years needs a closer look at its overall condition before you spend money on it.

### 2. How bad is the damage?

A creased shingle, a cracked pipe boot or loose flashing is a contained problem with a clear fix. Brittle shingles, widespread granule loss or hail bruising are condition problems that affect the whole roof.

### 3. How much of the roof is affected?

This is where many decisions get made. If the problem is in one small area, repair it. As the affected share grows to a full slope, and then to multiple slopes, repairs start to cost almost as much per square foot as new roofing, without the fresh start or the new warranty.

### 4. What's the leak history?

One leak with an obvious source is normal. Repeated leaks in different spots over the last few years mean the roof is failing in several places, and fixing them one at a time rarely ends well.

### 5. What shape is the decking in?

Soft, spongy or rotten decking changes the math. Damaged sheets have to be replaced before any new shingles go on, and if rot is widespread, a tear-off is usually the only practical way to reach it.

### 6. How long will you stay in the home?

If you plan to stay for many years, a replacement pays you back over a long time. If you're selling soon, a solid repair plus documentation may be enough, but keep in mind that buyers' inspectors and lenders pay close attention to older roofs.

### 7. Is insurance involved?

If a recent storm caused the damage, your insurance policy may cover some or all of a replacement. That can turn a "repair for now" decision into a "replace now" decision. More on that below.

## Repair or replace: a decision table

| Situation | Usually the better choice |
|---|---|
| Roof under ~15 years, damage in one spot | Repair |
| Roof under ~15 years, storm damage on several slopes | Inspection and insurance claim; possibly replacement |
| Roof 15 to 20 years, isolated problem, shingles still flexible | Repair, and start budgeting |
| Roof past ~20 years, wear across the whole roof | Replacement |
| First leak, clear source (pipe boot, flashing) | Repair |
| Repeated leaks in different places | Replacement, or at least a full assessment |
| Soft or rotten decking in a small area | Repair with decking replacement |
| Widespread soft decking or sagging | Replacement |
| Selling within a year or two, roof otherwise sound | Repair and keep records |

## How the cost math works

A repair nearly always has the smaller price tag. A replacement is a much bigger check: This Old House puts the average roof replacement in Mississippi at about **$7,763**, with typical prices ranging from **$6,164 to $18,621**. Your real number depends on roof size, pitch, complexity, materials and decking, and only an on-site estimate can tell you. Our [Southaven roof replacement cost guide](/roof-replacement-cost-southaven) breaks down what drives the price.

Instead of comparing the two prices head to head, compare **cost per year of protection**:

- A repair that costs a small fraction of a new roof and buys five or more good years is usually excellent value.
- A repair on a roof with only a couple of years left buys very little. If you'll need a new roof soon anyway, that money might be better put toward the replacement.
- Repeated repairs add up. Every service call carries setup costs, and on a steep or two-story home, safety setup alone can be a real part of the bill.

Also think about what a leak can cost. Wet insulation, stained drywall and mold cleanup are not part of any roofing estimate, but they're very real in a humid climate.

## Partial replacement: re-roofing one slope

Sometimes the honest answer falls in between: replace the damaged slope and leave the rest. This often comes up after a tree limb comes down on one side of the house, or when one slope faces the afternoon sun and has aged faster than the others.

**Pros:**

- Costs less than a full replacement
- Makes sense when the rest of the roof is young and in good shape
- Fixes a severely damaged area properly instead of patching it

**Cons:**

- **Shingle matching.** Even the same product and color won't look identical next to shingles that have weathered for years. GAF's own limited warranty notes that replacement shingles may not match because of normal weathering and manufacturing variation, and manufacturers discontinue colors.
- **Warranty questions.** A new slope has new materials while the rest of the roof keeps its original age. Premium manufacturer warranties often require a complete roof system; Owens Corning's Platinum Protection warranty, for example, requires the system to cover the entire structure. Ask how the manufacturer's warranty and the contractor's workmanship warranty apply, and get the answer in writing.
- **Two roofs aging on different clocks.** The old sections will still wear out on their original schedule, so you may face another project in a few years.

A partial replacement works best when the remaining roof has plenty of life left. On an older roof, it often just postpones the full job.

## When insurance changes the decision

After hail or high winds, the decision is no longer only yours. If an adjuster finds enough covered damage, your insurer may pay to replace damaged slopes or the whole roof, minus your deductible. That can make replacement the obvious choice even for a roof you would otherwise have repaired.

A few things to understand first:

- **Storm damage vs. wear.** Policies generally cover sudden damage from covered events, not age and deterioration.
- **Your deductible.** Some policies use a wind or hail deductible set as a percentage of your dwelling coverage. If the damage is small, it may not exceed it.
- **Replacement cost vs. actual cash value.** Some policies pay what it costs to replace the roof; others subtract depreciation for age. Some insurers also pay roof claims on a schedule based on the roof's age.
- **Cosmetic damage exclusions**, which are especially common for metal roofs.

Have the roof inspected and photographed before you file. Then read your policy, ask your agent, and, if you need help, the Mississippi Insurance Department offers consumer assistance. Public adjusters in Mississippi are licensed by the department. Our page on [roof insurance claims](/roof-insurance-claims) walks through the process.

## Questions to ask your roofer

An honest recommendation comes with reasons you can check. Ask:

1. **What exactly is wrong, and can you show me photos?**
2. **Is this damage localized, or is it a condition of the whole roof?**
3. **How many years do you think the roof has left if I repair it?**
4. **What would the repair cost, and what would replacement cost?** Ask for both in writing.
5. **Did you check the decking and the attic?**
6. **Does this look like storm damage or normal wear?**
7. **What warranty comes with the repair, and with the replacement?**
8. **Are you licensed?** Mississippi requires a state license from the Mississippi State Board of Contractors for residential roofing jobs over $10,000. You can verify licenses on the board's website.

## How to spot an upsell

Plenty of roofers give fair advice, but it pays to recognize the warning signs:

- **Replacement recommended on a young roof** with no photos showing widespread damage.
- **No repair option offered at all**, even for a problem in one spot.
- **Pressure tactics**, like a price that's only good today or a claim that your insurer "will drop you" if you don't act now.
- **Vague estimates** that don't name the shingle product, decking price, flashing or warranty.
- **Offers to waive or "cover" your deductible.** Your policy expects you to pay it; ask your agent if anyone suggests otherwise.
- **Door-to-door contractors after a storm** who ask you to sign over your claim before you've checked their license or references.
