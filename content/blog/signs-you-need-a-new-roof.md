Roofs rarely fail overnight. They wear out slowly, and the clues show up in your gutters, your attic and your ceilings long before water drips onto the floor. The trick is knowing which clues mean "fix this spot" and which mean "start planning a new roof."

This guide walks through nine signs that a Southaven roof may need replacing, what each one looks like, why it matters in our climate, and what to do about it. When you want a professional opinion, one call connects you with a local roofing contractor for a free, no-obligation inspection.

## How to check your roof safely from the ground

Please don't climb onto your roof. Shingles are slippery when dusty or damp, two-story homes are common in Southaven, and a fall is far more expensive than any roof. You can learn a surprising amount without leaving the ground:

- **Use binoculars** (or your phone's zoom) and walk all the way around the house. Look at each slope in good daylight.
- **Check the gutters and downspout outlets** for dark, sand-like granules, shingle pieces or dents.
- **Look in the attic** on a bright afternoon with a flashlight. Turn the light off for a minute and look for pinpoints of daylight, then turn it on and look for stains, damp insulation or dark wood.
- **Check ceilings and upper walls**, especially in closets and upstairs rooms you don't use often.
- **Walk the yard after storms** for shingle tabs, ridge caps or flashing pieces.

Anything that needs a closer look, like hail bruises or soft decking, is a job for a roofer with fall protection.

## Signs of age and wear

### 1. Your roof is approaching 20 years old (or more)

**What it looks like:** You may not see anything dramatic. The roof just looks a bit flat and faded, and you know (or suspect) it was installed around the time the house was built or bought.

**Why it matters in Southaven's climate:** Architectural shingles typically last roughly 20 to 30 years and three-tab shingles often less, but long, hot summers push most roofs toward the lower end of the range. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory notes that traditional dark roofs can reach 150°F or more in summer sun. Years of that heat dry out the asphalt.

**What to do:** Figure out the roof's real age from closing papers, a home inspection report or old receipts. Our guide to [how long a roof lasts in Mississippi](/blog/how-long-does-a-roof-last-mississippi) explains more. Past 20 years, schedule an inspection even if nothing is leaking.

### 2. Granules are piling up in the gutters

**What it looks like:** Handfuls of dark, gritty granules in the gutters or splashed out at the bottom of downspouts, and shingles that look patchy or "bald" from the ground.

**Why it matters here:** Granules shield the asphalt from UV rays. Once they're gone, the sun bakes the exposed asphalt quickly, and the brittle shingles that result have to shed the more than 50 inches of rain the Memphis area gets in a typical year.

**What to do:** A little grit on a new roof is fine. Heavy, ongoing granule loss on an older roof, or sudden loss right after a hailstorm, deserves an inspection.

### 3. Shingles are curling or cupping

**What it looks like:** Shingle edges turning up (cupping) or the middle of the shingle bowing up while the corners curl down (clawing). From the ground, the roof looks wavy or lumpy instead of flat.

**Why it matters here:** Curling is often a sign of heat aging, sometimes made worse by an attic that traps hot, moist air. Curled shingles lose their seal, so straight-line winds from spring thunderstorms can catch them and tear them off.

**What to do:** A few curled shingles can be replaced. Curling across whole slopes usually means the shingles have reached the end of their life, and a [roof replacement](/roof-replacement) is the realistic fix. Ask the roofer to check attic ventilation too.

## Signs of damage and leaks

### 4. Cracked, torn or missing shingles

**What it looks like:** Gaps in the shingle pattern, exposed black underlayment, shingle tabs in the yard, or horizontal crease lines where a tab was lifted by wind and folded back.

**Why it matters here:** Each missing or creased shingle is an open door for wind-driven rain. Older shingles also get brittle, so they crack when wind lifts them instead of flexing.

**What to do:** If damage is limited to a few spots on a younger roof, a repair is usually fine. If shingles crack or break when the roofer tries to lift them for a repair, that brittleness is a strong sign the whole roof is near the end.

### 5. Leaks keep coming back

**What it looks like:** A ceiling stain that returns after it was "fixed," or new stains showing up in different rooms.

**Why it matters here:** Heavy spring and summer downpours find every weak point. A roof that leaks in several places has usually worn out in more than one place, and each repair only addresses one of them.

**What to do:** Keep a simple record of dates, rooms and repair costs. A single leak often has a single cause (see [roof leak repair](/roof-leak-repair)). Repeated leaks in new places on an aging roof are one of the clearest reasons to stop patching and replace.

### 6. The roof deck is sagging

**What it looks like:** A ridge line that dips, a swayback look between rafters, or soft spots visible as low areas on a slope.

**Why it matters here:** Sagging usually means the decking (the plywood or OSB under the shingles) has absorbed water for a long time, or the structure has a problem. Wet wood in a humid climate weakens and can rot.

**What to do:** Treat this one as urgent and call for an inspection soon. Sagging decking can't be fixed with new shingles alone. The damaged sheets, and sometimes framing, need to be replaced, which is usually done as part of a tear-off.

## Signs from the attic and after storms

### 7. Daylight or water stains in the attic

**What it looks like:** Pinholes of light through the roof boards, dark streaks on rafters, stained or matted insulation, or rusty nail tips.

**Why it matters here:** Daylight means water can get in. Stains and rusty nails can come from leaks, but in a hot, humid attic they can also come from condensation when ventilation is poor. That distinction matters, because the fix is different.

**What to do:** Take photos and show them to the roofer. A good roofer will tell you whether you're looking at roof leaks, a ventilation problem, or both.

### 8. Moss, algae and what they really mean

**What it looks like:** Black or dark green streaks running down the roof (algae), or fuzzy green clumps, usually on shaded slopes under trees (moss).

**Why it matters here:** Warm, humid weather is ideal for algae. The Asphalt Roofing Manufacturers Association identifies *Gloeocapsa magma* as probably the most common culprit, and the streaks are **mostly a cosmetic issue**, not a sign your roof is failing. Moss is different: it holds moisture against the shingles and can lift their edges.

**What to do:** Don't pressure-wash. ARMA warns that high-pressure washing is likely to damage asphalt roofing. Algae alone is not a reason to replace a roof. Thick moss deserves a closer look at the shingles underneath, and trimming back overhanging branches helps.

### 9. Storm damage across several slopes

**What it looks like:** Missing tabs on several sides of the house, dented gutters, vents and downspouts after hail, and lots of granules washed out at once.

**Why it matters here:** Southaven's spring severe-storm season brings hail and straight-line winds. Hail can bruise shingles and crack the mat without leaving obvious holes, and that damage speeds up aging even if nothing leaks right away.

**What to do:** Get an inspection soon after the storm, before you file a claim. If damage covers multiple slopes, replacement may be partly covered by insurance. See [hail damage roof repair](/hail-damage-roof-repair) for what roofers look for.

## Quick self-check: repair or replace?

Use this as a starting point, not a final answer. An on-site inspection is the only way to know for sure.

| What you're seeing | Usually points to |
|---|---|
| Roof under ~15 years old with one damaged area | Repair |
| Roof past ~20 years with general wear | Plan for replacement |
| Heavy granule loss across the roof | Replacement likely |
| Curling or cupping on whole slopes | Replacement likely |
| A few missing or creased shingles after wind | Repair, if shingles are still flexible |
| One leak with a clear source (pipe boot, flashing) | Repair |
| Repeated leaks in different places | Replacement likely |
| Sagging deck or soft spots | Inspection right away; often replacement |
| Daylight through the roof boards | Inspection; repair or replace depending on extent |
| Black algae streaks only | Neither; cosmetic |
| Hail or wind damage on several slopes | Inspection and possible insurance claim |

For a deeper look at the trade-offs, read [roof repair vs. replacement](/blog/roof-repair-vs-replacement).

## What to do next

If you've checked a few boxes above, the next step is a professional [roof inspection](/roof-inspection). A thorough inspection should end with photos of each problem, an honest estimate of how many years the roof has left, and a written estimate for a repair, a replacement, or both.

A few tips as you get started:

- **Don't wait on active leaks.** Wet insulation and decking get worse quickly in humid weather.
- **Get the inspection before calling your insurer** if you suspect storm damage, so you know whether there's real damage to report.
- **Ask for photos of everything.** A trustworthy roofer is happy to show you what they found.
- **Compare written estimates** that spell out materials, decking, flashing and warranties.
