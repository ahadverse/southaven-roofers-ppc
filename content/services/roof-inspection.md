You don't need to wait for a leak to find out what shape your roof is in. A professional roof inspection shows you exactly what's happening up there: storm damage you can't see from the ground, worn flashing that's about to leak, ventilation problems that are cooking your shingles from below. In Southaven, where spring storms and summer heat take turns on your roof, an inspection is cheap insurance.

## When to get a roof inspection

- **After a hail storm or high winds.** Even if you don't see damage from the yard.
- **Before your insurance claim deadline** if a storm hit your area.
- **When your roof turns 15** (or sooner if it's a three-tab shingle roof).
- **Before buying a home.** A general home inspector may not walk the roof.
- **Before selling**, so you can address problems before a buyer's inspection does.
- **When you notice warning signs:** stains on ceilings, granules in gutters, shingles in the yard.

## Roofer, home inspector or adjuster: who does what

Three different people may look at your roof, and each has a different job:

| Who | Works for | Main purpose | What you get |
|---|---|---|---|
| Roofing contractor | You (often free for estimates) | Find problems and price the fix | Photos, a condition assessment and a written repair or replacement estimate |
| Home inspector | You, during a purchase (paid) | Review the whole house, roof included | A broad report that may recommend a roofer take a closer look |
| Insurance adjuster | Your insurance company | Decide what storm damage the policy covers | A scope of loss and a claim payment |

Knowing the difference helps you set expectations. An adjuster isn't there to tell you how many years your roof has left, and a home inspector usually won't price repairs. A roofer's inspection fills those gaps.

## What a thorough roof inspection covers

### Roof surface
- Missing, cracked, curling, cupped or creased shingles
- Hail bruises and granule loss, often checked in test squares on each slope
- Nail pops, exposed nails and sealant failures
- Moss, algae streaks and debris build-up

### Flashing and penetrations
- Step and counterflashing at walls and chimneys
- Pipe boots around plumbing vents
- Skylight seals
- Valleys, where two slopes meet

### Edges, gutters and ventilation
- Drip edge and fascia condition
- Gutters and downspouts (dents can indicate hail size; clogs cause water backup)
- Ridge vents, box vents, turbines and soffit intake vents

### Attic (when accessible)
- Water stains, wet insulation, mold or mildew
- Daylight through the decking
- Ventilation and signs of excessive heat or condensation
- Sagging or damaged rafters and decking

## What roofers watch for on Southaven roofs

Every climate wears roofs in its own way. In north Mississippi, a careful inspector pays extra attention to:

- **Heat damage from below.** Hot, humid summers with highs in the 90s push attic temperatures far higher. Blocked soffit vents or too little ridge exhaust can bake shingles and make them brittle years early.
- **Sun-facing slopes.** South- and west-facing slopes usually lose granules first, so they often decide how much life the whole roof has left.
- **Spring hail marks.** After April storms, a roofer will check soft metals like vents and gutters for dents, then look for matching hits on the shingles.
- **Wind-lifted tabs.** Straight-line winds break shingle seals along ridges, rakes and corners, even when nothing blows off.
- **Tree contact and shade.** In older neighborhoods with mature trees, such as Carriage Hills and Greenbrook, limbs that rub the roof scrape away granules, and constant shade keeps shingles damp enough for algae and moss.
- **Rubber pipe boots.** Mississippi sun dries and cracks them, and they're among the most common leak sources a roofer finds.

## What you should get afterward

A good inspection ends with more than "looks fine." Expect:

- **Photos** of every problem area
- **A clear explanation** of what's urgent, what can wait and what's just cosmetic
- **An estimate of remaining roof life**
- **A written estimate** for any recommended repairs or replacement
- **Honest advice about insurance.** Whether damage looks storm-related and worth reporting, or is normal wear

## How to get the most from your inspection

A little preparation makes the visit faster and the findings more useful:

- **Clear a path to the attic hatch** and move stored items so the roofer can see the underside of the decking.
- **Note where and when** you've seen stains, drips or musty smells, including which rooms and during what kind of rain.
- **Find your paperwork:** the year the roof was installed, the shingle brand if you know it, and any warranty or past repair invoices.
- **Write down recent storm dates** if you heard hail or saw high wind.
- **Secure pets** and unlock gates so the roofer can reach every side of the house.

Then ask a few direct questions before the roofer leaves:

1. How many years do you think this roof has left?
2. What needs attention now, and what can safely wait?
3. Does any of this look like storm damage, or is it normal wear?
4. Can I get the photos and findings in writing?
5. If I put off a repair, what's likely to happen next?

## Inspections after storms

After hail or high winds, an inspection is the first step before calling your insurer. A roofer can tell you whether there's meaningful storm damage, photograph it, and help you avoid filing a claim for damage that falls below your deductible. Because insurers generally expect prompt reporting, getting an inspection soon after the storm protects your options. ## Inspections for home buyers and sellers

Southaven's housing market moves quickly, and roofs are one of the most expensive items buyers ask about. If you're buying, a roof-specific inspection can reveal remaining life and needed repairs you can negotiate before closing. If you're selling, knowing the roof's condition ahead of time helps you price the home and avoid surprises late in the deal.

**Buyers** should also ask the sellers how old the roof is, whether it was replaced after a storm claim, and whether any manufacturer or workmanship warranty transfers to a new owner. Roof age matters for insurance too: some insurers ask about it when writing a new policy and may limit coverage or settle claims differently on older roofs, so call your agent before closing.

**Sellers** can head off tough negotiations by fixing obvious, inexpensive problems before listing, such as cracked pipe boots, a few missing shingles or loose flashing. Keep receipts and photos of the work, and have a copy of the inspection report ready to share. A buyer who can see what was checked and fixed is less likely to ask for a large credit.

## Spot problems between inspections

You can do a lot from the ground and inside your home. After every major storm, take a few minutes to:

- Walk around the house and look for shingles, granules or metal pieces in the yard
- Check gutters and downspouts for dents and granule build-up
- Look at ceilings, especially upstairs and in closets, for new stains
- Peek in the attic with a flashlight for water trails or daylight
