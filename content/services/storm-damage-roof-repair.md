## Storm season in Southaven

Severe weather can hit DeSoto County in any month, but the pattern is fairly predictable:

- **Spring (roughly March through May)** is the main severe-storm season, with squall lines, hail, damaging straight-line winds and tornado watches. Storm reports compiled from NOAA data show April has historically been the busiest month for hail near Southaven.
- **Summer** brings fast-building afternoon thunderstorms fueled by heat and humidity. They're often short, but they can pack strong gusts. On July 13, 2025, a storm brought down trees across Southaven without any hail at all.
- **Fall and winter** are usually quieter, but strong cold fronts can still produce damaging wind, and hail has been reported near Southaven even in January.

Older neighborhoods with tall, mature trees, such as Carriage Hills and Greenbrook, tend to see more limb damage in wind storms. Newer subdivisions with smaller trees get less of that but more exposure to wind across open lots.

## Types of storm damage roofers find in Southaven

### Wind damage
Straight-line winds can be as destructive as a weak tornado. They lift shingle tabs, break the adhesive seals that hold shingles down, and peel shingles off entirely, often starting at the ridge, the edges and the corners where uplift is strongest. Creased shingles that are still on the roof are damaged too: once the seal breaks, the next storm can take them.

### Hail damage
Hail bruises and fractures shingles, knocks off the protective granules, and dents metal vents, gutters and flashing. Hail damage often isn't visible from the yard but shortens a roof's life significantly. See [hail damage roof repair](/hail-damage-roof-repair) for details.

### Falling trees and limbs
Southaven's mature neighborhoods have plenty of large oaks and pines. During high winds, limbs can puncture the roof deck or crush part of the structure.

### Water intrusion
Any of the above can let rain into the attic. Wind-driven rain can also get past flashing and vents that normally stay dry.

## What to do after a storm damages your roof

1. **Make sure everyone is safe.** Stay away from downed power lines and out of rooms with sagging ceilings.
2. **Prevent further damage.** If water is coming in, contain it and ask a roofer about [emergency tarping](/emergency-roof-repair). Most policies expect homeowners to take reasonable steps to prevent more damage.
3. **Document everything.** Take date-stamped photos and video of the roof (from the ground), siding, gutters, windows, fences, cars and any interior damage. Save receipts for tarps or emergency repairs.
4. **Get a professional inspection.** A roofer can get on the roof safely, find damage you can't see and photograph it. This helps you decide whether the damage is worth a claim.
5. **Contact your insurance company** to report the damage and get a claim number.
6. **Meet the adjuster.** Having your roofer at the adjuster's inspection helps make sure all damage is noted.

## A safe post-storm walkaround

Once the weather has cleared and it's safe to go outside, a ten-minute walk around the house tells you a lot without ever touching a ladder:

| What you see | What it may mean |
|---|---|
| Shingle pieces or ridge caps in the yard | Wind has torn shingles loose; check the slope facing the wind first |
| Dark, flat patches where shingles used to be | Missing shingles exposing underlayment or bare decking |
| Shingle tabs lifted or flapping | Broken seals that can blow off in the next storm |
| Dented gutters, downspouts or AC unit fins | Hail; the size of the dents hints at hail size |
| Damage on one side of the house only | Wind-driven hail or wind direction; tell your roofer which side |
| Limbs resting on the roof | Possible punctures or cracked decking under the debris |
| New stains on upstairs ceilings | Water is already getting in; arrange a tarp quickly |

Photograph anything you find, with a wide shot showing the house and a close-up. Leave the climbing and close inspection to the roofer.

## How insurance usually handles storm damage

Every policy is different, so read yours and talk with your agent. In general:

- **Wind and hail are covered perils** on most standard homeowners policies, but some policies carry a separate wind/hail deductible, sometimes a percentage of your dwelling coverage rather than a flat dollar amount.
- **Replacement cost value (RCV)** policies typically pay the actual cash value first, then release the withheld depreciation after the work is completed.
- **Actual cash value (ACV)** policies pay what the roof was worth considering its age, which can be far less than the cost of a new roof.
- **Wear and tear isn't covered.** Insurers look for damage from the storm itself, which is why a professional inspection with clear photos matters.

## Beware of storm chasers

After big storms, out-of-town contractors often flood the Mid-South. Some do fine work, but others collect deposits and disappear, do rushed installs, or leave before warranty problems show up. Protect yourself:

- Verify the contractor's **Mississippi State Board of Contractors license** for residential roofing jobs over $10,000
- Ask for proof of **liability and workers' compensation insurance**
- Get a **local address and phone number**, and check reviews and references
- **Never sign** a contract or "assignment of benefits" on the spot
- **Be wary of anyone offering to "cover" your deductible.** That can amount to insurance fraud
- **Don't pay in full upfront**

## Storm damage repair vs. replacement

If damage is limited to a few shingles or one slope, a repair may be enough. When wind or hail has damaged multiple slopes, or the shingles can no longer be repaired without breaking the ones around them (common on older roofs), a full replacement is often the right answer, and is frequently what an insurance claim ends up covering. A roofer can explain the difference after inspecting the roof.

## Get your roof ready for the next storm

You can't stop a storm, but you can make your roof a smaller target before spring arrives:

- **Trim limbs** that hang over the roof or rub against it. Have a licensed tree service handle large limbs near the house or power lines.
- **Clean gutters and downspouts** so heavy rain drains instead of backing up under the roof edge.
- **Fix lifted or missing shingles now.** Shingles with broken seals are the first to go in the next wind event.
- **Take "before" photos** of each side of your roof every spring. They make it much easier to show what changed after a storm.
- **Know your deductible.** Check whether your policy has a separate wind/hail deductible so you aren't surprised after a loss.
- **Save a local roofer's number** before you need it, so you aren't choosing from whoever knocks on the door.
